# Foundations: intent, frame, colour and light

Keep the truthfulness the picture's use requires (documentary, product, likeness); everything else is a creative choice made deliberately.

## Intent, attention and frame

Describe the particular picture in one sentence: what draws attention first, what follows, and the mood. Ground it in visible light, gesture, form, rhythm, relationship or place. Name what must stay recognisable or accurate: expression, markings, product colour, proportions, weather. Fix the largest obstacle to that intent first.

Three quick checks make it concrete: view the frame in greyscale to find subject and background of equal brightness that merge; squint or view it small to see the three to five main tonal masses; rank what pulls attention (brightest area, strongest local contrast, sharpest detail, most saturated colour, faces and eyes). A bright corner, a sign, a red garment or a high-contrast branch can outcompete the subject: quiet the competitor before pushing every subject slider. Background can carry scale, habitat and story; a cleaner crop costs that context.

Check the edges for tangencies, severed limbs, cut handles, stray reflections and distracting fragments. Space toward a gaze or motion, centred symmetry, negative space and deliberate tightness are choices with different effects; a thirds grid is one possibility. Choose the aspect ratio for the picture's structure; a shared series ratio helps presentation but can cost individual compositions, so see that cost before imposing it. Establish a reliable horizontal or vertical before straightening: a sloping street, a curved bank or a leaning body is not one.

**Comparing crops.** Keep the current frame as the baseline and test a purposeful alternative: reduce a competing edge, give the eye or gesture useful space, strengthen a diagonal, symmetry or reflection. Thirds and golden-ratio lines suggest positions; keep a guide only when the visible relationships improve. Check the complete subject, wings and tail, gaze room and reflection. Compare at the intended display size and check critical detail again: fewer pixels and a larger displayed subject can expose softness even when the composition improves. An unchanged crop is a valid result.

## Colour and tone

Judge hue, saturation and lightness together with a colour's area and position. Choose the strongest useful separation: light/dark, warm/cool, vivid/quiet or hue. Analogous colours unify; opposing colours separate. Colour symbolism depends on the scene and the viewer.

Name the control colours and how far each may move: skin, white fur or feathers, food, fabric, neutral walls, packaging. White balance sets the global warm/cool and green/magenta balance; a neutral eyedropper needs a truly neutral, unclipped area.

The profile interprets the RAW's tone and colour: compare foundations before complex grading, and revisit the treatment if you change profile later. HSL moves colour families and reaches subject and background alike; Color Grading targets tonal ranges; RGB curves change tone and colour by another mechanism. None of them is the same as raising white balance. Keep a tonal hierarchy: recover highlights that hold information, keep depth in blacks where it serves, and do not make every shadow equally visible. Matte blacks reduce dark separation; strong Dehaze can erase atmospheric depth and shift colour.

## Light, material and alternatives

When the intent is natural, support the existing light locally: lighten a face, restrain a competing stone, shape a material's reflection. Strong relighting is a creative choice: name it honestly. Material priorities differ: skin, rock, feather, ceramic and a smooth background each want different texture, sharpening and noise treatment.

When the direction is open, compare two distinct ideas, and a third only for an independent purpose. Show them at similar size and broadly similar brightness, and name the benefit and cost of each (clearer face but weaker place; calmer greens but less colour variety). For a series, match relationships in contrast, highlights, saturation and texture while letting real light and white balance differ. Stop when the intent reads and the required qualities survive.

## Output

Check the whole frame, the important detail and the actual delivery size: reductions alter fine lines, text, noise and sharpening. Use the destination's size and colour requirements; sRGB is a common web choice, not a substitute for a requested specification. For print use the lab's paper profile and soft-proof where available; a screen simulation cannot guarantee another display or a print.
